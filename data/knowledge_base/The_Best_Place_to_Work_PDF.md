About the book
In *The Best Place to Work*, award-winning psychologist
Ron Friedman, Ph.D., invites readers on an engaging
exploration of the science behind workplace excellence.
Drawing on groundbreaking research from motivation,
creativity, behavioral economics, neuroscience, and
management, Friedman uncovers the surprising strategies that
lead to success in the modern work environment. Through
captivating stories and innovative insights, he reveals how to
foster smarter thinking, ignite innovation, and enhance
performance at all organizational levels. Discover how
learning to think like a hostage negotiator can resolve
conflicts, why a simple fish bowl can spark creativity, and
how strategic distractions can lead to better decision-making.
With eye-opening anecdotes and practical recommendations,
this book equips both employees and executives with the tools
to transform any organization into an exceptional workplace,
regardless of its size or resources.
About the author
Ron Friedman is a renowned organizational psychologist and
expert in the field of workplace dynamics, known for his
ability to translate complex psychological principles into
practical strategies for enhancing employee engagement and
productivity. With a rich academic background and extensive
experience consulting for various organizations, Friedman
draws on both empirical research and real-world applications
to understand what makes workplaces thrive. His insights have
been featured in major publications, and he is a sought-after
speaker who empowers leaders to create environments where
individuals can excel. In "The Best Place to Work," Friedman
distills his expertise into actionable advice for fostering
positive organizational cultures that support both employee
well-being and business success.

Success Is Overrated
Why Great Workplaces Reward Failure
Silas Johnson, an unexpected baseball pitcher, became
famous after striking out Babe Ruth. Despite Johnson's
below-average career statistics, his moment of success
became legendary, highlighting how failure can coexist with
greatness. Similarly, Albert Ellis faced rejection while
attempting to connect with women but found that facing
failure ultimately improved his confidence and social skills.
The Defining Feature of Renowned Artists, Star
Athletes, and Successful Organizations
Research by Dean Keith Simonton reveals that creative
geniuses produce more work, including failures, which leads
to greater success. Artists like Shakespeare and musicians
like Beethoven created many works, only a fraction of which
were masterpieces. This reflects the principle that higher
output increases the likelihood of groundbreaking ideas.
The Importance of Embracing Failure in Pursuit of
Success
The chapter emphasizes that failure is a crucial element of
growth, illustrated by stories of famous figures like Thomas
Edison and Babe Ruth. Successful athletes often seek
challenges that may lead to failure, as this promotes learning
and skill mastery. Companies like Google embrace failures
as essential for innovation.
Spanx and the Secret of Success
Sara Blakely credits her success in the shapewear industry to
her upbringing, which encouraged her to view failure as part
of learning. Her father's dinner question about daily failures
fostered a mindset that saw initiative and risk-taking as paths
to growth rather than signs of weakness.
How to Spark Creativity
Creativity often thrives in a motivational environment that
rewards exploration, as opposed to one that punishes
mistakes. An experiment demonstrated that individuals in an
approach state (motivated by potential gains) performed
better in creative tasks than those in an avoidance state (fear
of losses).
Why Successful Teams Make More Mistakes
Research shows that teams with strong relationships report
more mistakes, as a psychologically safe environment allows
for acknowledgment and learning from errors. This contrasts
with teams that hide failures, preventing growth and
improvement.
The Right Way to Reward Failure
Organizations like Grey Advertising and Merck underscore
the importance of recognizing failure as a means for growth.
They establish awards or incentives to encourage risk-taking,
leading to innovation rather than fear-based conservatism.
The Lessons of Failure
Action Items for Managers
- Reward attempts, not just outcomes.
- Mine failures for opportunities.
- Play the long game in fostering innovation.
Action Items for Emerging Leaders
- Reflect on daily failures as growth opportunities.
- Anticipate the J Curve of progress.
- Seek environments that value continuous learning and
experimentation.
Example
Key Point:Embrace failure as a path to growth.
Example:Imagine you're working on a creative project
at your job. You pitch a bold idea, but it flops
spectacularly. Instead of feeling defeated, you reflect on
what went wrong, seeking feedback and learning from
the experience. In this moment, you realize that the risk
you took—though leading to failure—has equipped you
with valuable insights that will make your future
projects more robust and innovative. Just like Silas
Johnson's unexpected strike against Babe Ruth, your
failures serve as stepping stones to eventual success,
redefining your perspective on achievement.
Critical Thinking
Key Point:The paradigm of failure as a stepping
stone to success is contentious and
context-dependent.
Critical Interpretation:While the chapter advocates for a
workplace culture that embraces failure as essential for
growth, it's crucial to recognize that not all
environments afford the luxury of experimentation.
Critics may argue that in high-stakes industries, the
consequences of failure can be catastrophic, with
examples in healthcare or aviation demonstrating that a
more cautious approach may be prudent (Woods et al.,
2016). Furthermore, fostering a culture that exclusively
celebrates risk-taking may inadvertently overlook the
value of meticulous planning and incremental
improvement, cautioning against the notion that failure
is a universally beneficial catalyst.

How Office Design Shapes Our Thinking
Office design significantly influences our cognitive abilities,
with new studies revealing that aspects like room height and
color affect creativity and focus. Companies like Google and
Intel are investing in spaces that enhance innovation, based

environment and thought processes.
A Brief History of Office Design
Robert Propst's Action Office aimed to revolutionize office
layouts but ended up leading to the modern cubicle, which
failed to provide privacy and comfort. Despite its commercial
success, Propst lamented its unintended consequences.
Current trends show that both cubicles and open spaces come
with drawbacks, making optimal workspace design a
challenge.
The Caveman’s Guide to Building a Better Office
Modern preferences for office design can be traced back to
our evolutionary history. Environments that offer views and
natural elements contribute to well-being and productivity.
Research shows that proximity to nature, access to natural
light, and personalized spaces lead to higher job satisfaction
and enhanced performance.
Using Space to Tell a Story
Workspaces can reflect organizational values and improve
employee engagement. Companies should design their
environments to convey their unique messages, fostering a
sense of belonging and purpose among employees.
Touchpoints within the workplace, including lobbies, break
rooms, and meeting spaces, should align with the company’s
identity.
The Lessons of Telecommuting: Why Employees Are
Often More Productive at Home
Telecommuting often results in higher productivity due to
factors like reduced distractions and a comfortable
environment. Insights from home workspaces should inform
office designs, creating spaces that enhance focus and allow
for personalization.
Caves and Campfires
An ideal workspace incorporates diverse environments that
cater to varying employee needs. A campus-style layout,
offering areas for both focused work and collaboration,
supports cognitive demands and enhances performance.
Flexible design helps build trust and empowers employees,
ultimately leading to greater productivity.
The Lessons of Workplace Design: Action Items for
Managers and Emerging Leaders
Organizations should strategically design workspaces with
specific tasks in mind while considering psychological
comfort. Enhancing workplace experiences can improve
employee engagement and productivity, rooted in
evolutionary insights and effective design practices.

What Happy Workplaces Can Learn from a Casino
Introduction: Personal Experience in a Casino
At nineteen, the author experiences both the thrill and
volatility of gambling during a Caribbean cruise. Despite
initially winning, a final impulsive bet leads to losing all
gains, illustrating how emotions can override rational
decision-making.
What Casinos Understand
Casinos utilize psychological strategies to enhance the
gambling experience and encourage risk-taking. These
methods include using chips instead of cash to minimize the
pain of losses, using electronic units to obscure real money
lost, and creating stimulating environments with lights and
sounds to influence gambler behavior.
The Connection to Workplaces
While risk-taking isn’t typically encouraged in professional
settings, promoting happiness among employees can lead to
greater effectiveness. Happy individuals are more
collaborative, creative, and resilient. The text emphasizes
embedding triggers within the workplace that foster positive
emotions.
Challenges in Sustaining Happiness
Humans generally struggle to maintain happiness over time
due to a concept known as the happiness baseline where
positive emotions diminish following achievements.
Adaptation occurs even after significant positive experiences,
suggesting the need for ongoing positive stimuli.
Insights for Promoting Workplace Happiness
1.
Frequency Over Size
Smaller, frequent positive experiences are more effective in
sustaining happiness than larger, infrequent events.
2.
Importance of Variety
Novel experiences can help delay adaptation, making
positive events feel more rewarding and keeping employees
engaged.
3.
Power of Unexpected Pleasures
Surprises amplify enjoyment and create lasting memories,
suggesting organizations use positive surprises to enhance
employee experience.
4.
Prioritize Experiences Over Objects
Investing in shared experiences yields greater satisfaction
than spending on material goods.
5.
Subconscious Influences
Scent and music can unconsciously affect mood,
highlighting the importance of a pleasant sensory
environment.
6.
Cultivating Gratitude
Encouraging gratitude can help maintain a positive
outlook, improving overall satisfaction and cooperation.
The Dual Nature of Happiness
The author reflects on the importance of acknowledging both
positive and negative emotions. Overemphasis on happiness
can lead to complacency and mistakes. Moderate
dissatisfaction might drive achievement and improvement,
and workplaces should promote authenticity and allow for
varied emotional experiences.
Action Items for Managers and Leaders
- Plan happiness strategies around specific activities to
enhance group dynamics.
- Implement small, frequent benefits rather than larger, rare
rewards.
- Encourage variety in tasks to maintain engagement and
satisfaction.
- Focus on gratitude practices to foster a supportive
environment among team members.
By understanding these principles, organizations can create
happier, more productive workplaces that effectively balance
and nurture emotional well-being.

- Studies show that friends collaborate better than

- Feeling connected increases motivation due to social

- Loneliness adversely affects physical and mental health,

- Research indicates that loneliness among employees

Creating Opportunities for Friendships
- Organizations can foster friendships through intentional
practices.

- Onboarding should not only focus on job competence but

- Utilize personal interests during introductions to spark

Strategies for Sustaining Workplace Relationships
- Promote informal social activities to enhance bonds,
including after-work gatherings that encourage shared
experiences.
- Establish superordinate goals that require cooperation
among employees from different departments to build
friendships.
Fostering a Supportive Community
- Recognize the value of social networks in enhancing mental
health and job satisfaction.
- Encourage celebratory practices for personal milestones and
provide support during challenging times to strengthen
workplace relationships.

can harm workplace culture.

concerns, and facilitate meaningful conversations.

mind.

workplace and seek common goals when conflicts arise.
- Be cautious of relying on gossip as a means of connection;
prioritize authentic relationships instead.

workplace community, enhancing collaboration and job
satisfaction.

Better Than Money
*What Games Can Teach Us About Motivation*
In Chapter 7, the author explores how the psychological
mechanisms behind video games can be applied to enhance
employee motivation at work. He narrates a personal
experience witnessing students captivated by the game Dance

The One Workplace Reward That Feels Better Than
a Raise
The chapter points out that while many would wish for a
salary increase if they could change anything about their
jobs, research shows that income has a limited long-term
impact on job satisfaction. Instead, respect and recognition
from peers greatly influence happiness and engagement
levels, as seen in studies involving MBA students and
prestigious awards.
Recognition, Feedback, and the Key to a Longer
Life
Receiving recognition is linked to better health and sustained
motivation; those recognized tend to live longer. In contrast,
a lack of recognition can lead to burnout and high turnover
rates. Feedback enhances competence and engagement,
emphasizing that meaningful compliments promote
motivation.
How Not to Recognize Employees
Despite acknowledging the importance of recognition, many
managers fail in delivering it effectively due to misplaced
priorities or outdated practices like annual performance
reviews. Overly frequent or insincere praise can diminish its
value.
The Science of Praise
Effective recognition should be immediate, specific, focused
on behaviors rather than traits, public, and emphasize adding
positives rather than removing negatives. This type of
feedback is essential for maintaining engagement.
The Busy Manager's Guide to Appreciation
Managers are often too busy to offer recognition, but
peer-to-peer recognition can help fill this gap and foster a
sense of connection among employees. Tools like workplace
social media can facilitate this process.
The Secret to Making Any Job Feel Meaningful
Connecting employees with the impact of their work fosters
meaning and motivation. Research demonstrates that
telemarketers improved performance significantly after
meeting scholarship recipients whose lives they had affected.
Why You Should Complicate Your Workers’ Lives
To achieve flow, engagement requires tasks that present
appropriate challenges. Organizations should prioritize
progressive difficulty rather than minimizing complexity, as
boring work leads to disengagement.
How to Build a Solutions Incubator
Like athletes, employees benefit from coaching to enhance
performance. Peer-to-peer coaching encourages collaboration
and accountability while helping to clarify objectives.
What Angry Birds Can Teach You About Creating
an Extraordinary Workplace

continuous improvement.

How Thinking Like a Hostage Negotiator Can Make You
More Persuasive, Influential, and Motivating**
Introduction: The Lindhurst High School Shooting
On May 1, 1992, Eric Houston, a disillusioned young man,
stormed Lindhurst High School, resulting in a tragic standoff
with police and the hostage-taking of eighty-five students.
This chapter uses this incident as a lens to explore the
concepts of negotiation, persuasion, and effective
communication.
Understanding Communication and Relationships
Effective communication is essential in various contexts,
including medical and sales industries. Studies show that
most patients sue doctors not due to malpractice but because
they felt unheard, illustrating how deteriorating relationships
can lead to conflict. Similarly, in sales, listening rather than
speaking enhances trust and long-term relationships with
clients.
The Importance of Listening in Influence
Influence in the workplace relies heavily on listening skills
rather than just speaking abilities. Research indicates that
engaged listening leads to better relationships and enhances a
manager's ability to motivate and retain employees. The lack
of listened concern from managers often sends a message
that employees’ opinions are undervalued.
Active Listening Techniques
Active listening is a critical skill that goes beyond passive
hearing. It involves:
- Mental presence: Focusing entirely on what is being said.
- Avoiding interruptions: Letting the speaker finish without
interjecting.
- Demonstrating engagement: Adopting an open and curious
body posture.
- Paraphrasing and validation: Reflecting the speaker's words
to deepen understanding and acknowledgment of their
feelings.
Defusing Arguments
In emotionally charged conflicts, it is essential to disentangle
task-related disagreements from relational concerns. This can
be achieved by using the PEARLS technique (Partnership,
Empathy, Acknowledgment, Respect, Legitimation, Support)
to focus on relationship-building rather than becoming
adversarial.
The Role of Chuck Tracy’s Negotiation Techniques
Chuck Tracy's successful negotiations with Eric Houston
exemplified the power of listening and empathy. By allowing
Houston to express himself and focusing on their shared
goals, Tracy managed to persuade him to surrender
peacefully. Tracy avoided confrontation and established a
rapport that facilitated open communication.
Lessons on Building Workplace Relationships
The chapter emphasizes several lessons:
1. Listening is a critical motivational tool and the foundation
of influential communication.
2. Disagreements can exist without negatively impacting
relationships; thus, attention to relational dynamics is crucial.
3. When seeking to change behavior, focus more on listening
than speaking.
Action Items for Managers
- Shrink your talking-to-listening ratio.
- Create an environment where employees feel their
contributions are valued.
- Focus on asking questions rather than having all the
answers.
Action Items for Emerging Leaders
- Engage in less combative arguments and prioritize
relationship-building.
- Avoid shift responses that distract from the speaker's
experience.
- Incorporate PEARLS statements into routine conversations
to strengthen connections.
In conclusion, the principles derived from hostage
negotiation can significantly improve workplace
communication and relationships, ultimately leading to more
engaged and motivated teams. Listening is a universal
language of acceptance and a powerful tool for influence and
collaboration.

Chapter 10: Seeing What Others Don’t
Introduction
The chapter explores how biases and blind spots during the
hiring process can obscure candidates' true potential,
illustrated by the blind audition process in orchestras,
specifically the story of Megan Prokes, a successful classical
violinist.
The Blind Audition Process
1.
Background
: Historically, female musicians faced bias during auditions.
Blind auditions, introduced by the Boston Symphony
Orchestra in 1952, involved concealing musicians' identities
to focus solely on their performances.
2.
Results
: This approach significantly increased women's
representation in orchestras, demonstrating that unconscious
biases can lead to incorrect assumptions about candidates
based on gender or appearance.
The Problem with Interviewing
1.
First Impressions
: Overestimating the value of initial judgments leads
interviewers to form biases based on appearance,
attractiveness, and other irrelevant traits.
2.
Cognitive Pitfalls
: Common biases include favoring candidates who remind us
of ourselves and misjudging warmth and competence, often
assuming they are inversely related.
Lying in Interviews
1.
Deception
: Job interviews create incentives for candidates to embellish
qualifications. Many applicants lie or stretch the truth during
interviews, often without realizing it.
2.
Difficulty of Detecting Lies
: Interviewers are generally poor at spotting lies, often more
confident in their judgments than justified.
Improving Hiring Decisions
1.
Streamlining Recruitment
: Companies like Google demonstrate the efficacy of
innovative recruitment methods that attract top talent by
engaging their desired candidates rather than waiting for
them to apply.
2.
Candidate Pool Quality
: Enhancing the applicant pool through employee referrals
generally yields better candidates due to pre-established
relationships and shared characteristics.
Reducing Interview Bias
1.
Structured Interviews
: Implementing blind audition techniques and structured
evaluations minimizes biases by concentrating on
job-relevant competencies.
2.
Behavioral and Situational Judgment Questions
: These types of questions provide insight into candidates’
actual past behaviors and hypothetical responses to relevant
scenarios.
What to Listen for in Interviews
1.
Emotional Tone
: Understanding candidates’ emotional outlook can provide
valuable insights beyond their qualifications and experiences.
2.
Indicator Traits
: Negative emotional responses, such as victimization or
excessive sarcasm, can be red flags indicating future
challenges in their roles.
Cultural Fit Considerations
1.
Diversity vs. Similarity
: While cultural fit is significant, diversity can enhance
performance by challenging groupthink and encouraging
innovative thinking.
2.
Finding the Right Balance
: Organizations should consider the importance of cultural fit
alongside the benefits of diverse perspectives, especially in
creative and adaptive environments.
Concluding Thoughts
The process of hiring should be viewed critically, as
cognitive biases and superficial judgments can severely cloud
decision-making. By implementing structured and thoughtful
hiring practices, organizations can improve their chances of
selecting the most capable candidates, leading to better
overall performance.
Action Items for Managers
1. Use blind audition techniques to focus on relevant
candidate skills.
2. Engage employees in the recruitment process to leverage
their networks.
3. Be aware of cultural fit but strive for diversity in hiring to
foster innovation.
Action Items for Emerging Leaders
1. Leverage personal networks to recommend candidates.

abilities.

be unjustly judged as less capable.

evaluations to focus purely on skills and performance—an
approach that can be adopted by organizations beyond music.

process?

conduct blind hiring when possible, utilize work samples,

biases.

What role does emotional tone play in interviews?

their fit for the role.

be cautious of hiring only similar candidates, as this can lead
to groupthink and inhibit innovation. A diverse team can
foster more effective decision-making.

interviews to enhance decision-making?
Answer:Managers should collect individual feedback from
interviewers before a group discussion to prevent bias from
influencing overall impressions. Follow-up with feedback to
help interviewers improve their evaluation skills.
